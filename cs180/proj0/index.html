Becoming Friends with Your Camera

Three small experiments on the relationship between perspective, focal length, and the center of projection. Every photo was taken on an iPhone.

Part 1

Selfie: The Wrong Way vs. The Right Way

The first photo was taken at arm’s length with no zoom. The second was taken from several feet away, zoomed in so that the face fills roughly the same area of the frame.

Why does this happen?

Perspective distortion depends only on where the camera is, not on how far it is zoomed in. When the camera is a foot from my face, the tip of my nose is noticeably closer to the lens than my ears are, so the nose projects much larger relative to the rest of the head. Stepping back makes the relative depth differences across the face tiny compared to the distance to the camera, so every feature is scaled by nearly the same amount. Zooming in afterward just crops and magnifies that flatter, more faithful projection.

Part 2

Architectural Perspective Compression

Same experiment in reverse, on the Valley Life Sciences Building at Berkeley. First a zoomed-in shot from far away, then a wide shot after walking up so the facade appears roughly the same size.

Why does this happen?

This is the same effect as the selfie, just applied to a subject with a lot of depth. From far away, the near and far corners of the building are at almost the same distance from the camera, so they project to nearly the same size and the depth is compressed. Up close, the near corner might be half as far from the camera as the far corner, so it appears twice as large. The strong size gradient across the facade is what our brains read as depth. Zoom only changes the magnification of the image; moving the camera changes the geometry.

Part 3

The Dolly Zoom

The “Vertigo shot”: move the camera backward while zooming in so the subject stays the same size in the frame. Eight stills of a Labubu on my windowsill, combined into an animated GIF.

Why does this happen?

Moving the camera back shrinks everything, but it shrinks nearby objects much faster than distant ones. Zooming in then scales the whole image up uniformly. If the zoom is chosen so the near subject returns to its original size, the far background, which shrank by a much smaller factor, ends up magnified well beyond its original size. The background appears to rush toward the subject while the subject sits still, and the field of view narrows, which is the disorienting feeling of the effect.
